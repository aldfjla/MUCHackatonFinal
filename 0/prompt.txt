  **1. "What is BATNA? How to Find Your Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement"** (PON, adapted from G. Subramanian). Full text, ~480 words.
  - BATNA is what you do if no deal happens. It decides whether to accept, how much leverage you have, and when to walk away.
  - Negotiators fixate on the offer in front of them and forget their alternatives.
  - Before comparing, translate both options into truly comparable terms. A headline price can hide worse coverage, exclusions or definitions.
  - Example: an insurance quote 30% cheaper turned out worse once fine print was compared. The apparent BATNA was weaker than it looked.
  - A BATNA is only as strong as your understanding of it. Misjudging it makes you reject good deals or accept bad ones.
  - Treat BATNA as ongoing: use realistic (not hypothetical) alternatives, compare like for like, update as information arrives.
  
  **2. "Six Guidelines for 'Getting to Yes'"** (K. Shonk). Full text, ~900 words (excluding comments).
  - Handle the people problem directly; take the other side's perspective, listen actively, clarify misunderstandings.
  - Look past stated positions to interests; ask "why is this important" and share your own interests so trades across issues appear.
  - Let emotions be expressed and named calmly; take breaks when heated.
  - Show appreciation: understanding and recognizing merit signals respect without agreeing, which lowers defenses.
  - Phrase messages constructively and personally (own your view); blame triggers defensiveness.
  - Don't mirror hard stances or attacks ("negotiation jujitsu"): answer with questions, interests, mutual-gain options, objective standards.
  - The article's own list is six; options and objective standards appear only in the reader comments (the book covers them).
  
  **3. "Principled Negotiation: Focus on Interests to Create Value"** (K. Shonk). Full text, ~800 words.
  - Hard vs. soft is a false choice; the third path is solving the problem on its merits.
  - Four elements: separate people from problem, interests over positions, invent options, objective criteria.
  - Positions are surface demands; interests are the needs behind them (siblings' party example resolved by costs vs. prep time).
  - Brainstorm before committing, suspend judgment, and trade across issues. Options include contingencies, conditions, timing, performance incentives.
  - Agree in advance on neutral standards (market value, benchmarks, expert opinion) to cut friction.
  - The goal is a deal better than your BATNA, not any deal. Strengthen your alternatives; walking away is disciplined, not failure.
  
  **4. "BATNA and Other Sources of Power at the Negotiation Table"** (PON Staff; Galinsky and Magee research). Full text, ~330 words.
  - Three power sources: a strong BATNA, role power (title/position), psychological power (feeling powerful).
  - A strong BATNA clarifies options, builds confidence, enables walking away (e.g. buyer finds another equally liked house).
  - Role power means you sometimes defer to high-status counterparts.
  - Psychological power can be raised just by recalling a time you had power, and improves outcomes.
  - Power from any source has similar effects; against a powerful counterpart, raise yours on all three levels.
  - A reader comment (not authoritative) adds weakening the other side's BATNA via market knowledge, common in procurement.
